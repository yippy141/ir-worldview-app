# V21 Reverse-Coded Item Candidates

Draft candidates for editorial review. Each item is written so agreement would lower the named dimension score. Proposed IDs are intentionally separate from the current bank.

**Editorial status:** `SELECTED` and `REWRITE` mark the approved scoring-v2 slate landed in `foundation.v2.json`.

## Security competition

### SELECTED — `v21_sc_rev_01`

- **Dimension:** `securityCompetition`
- **Text:** Major powers can build durable peace when each side accepts limits on military advantage.
- **Advocate:** Charles Kupchan identifies accommodation and reciprocal restraint as routes from rivalry to stable peace.
- **Misread risk:** Respondents may read “limits” as formal arms control, although the claim also covers tacit restraint.

### SELECTED — `v21_sc_rev_02`

- **Dimension:** `securityCompetition`
- **Text:** A rival's sustained restraint can provide credible evidence that its peaceful intentions will last.
- **Advocate:** Robert Jervis shows how restraint and reassurance can reduce fear and reveal peaceful intentions.
- **Misread risk:** Some may treat restraint as proof of weakness or temporary tactics, making “sustained” carry too much weight.

### Candidate — `v21_sc_rev_03`

- **Dimension:** `securityCompetition`
- **Text:** Deep economic ties can make war among major powers a costly and unattractive policy choice.
- **Advocate:** Richard Rosecrance argues that trading states can gain prosperity and security through commerce, reducing the gains from conquest.
- **Misread risk:** Respondents may answer from current supply-chain debates instead of their general view of war incentives.

### Candidate — `v21_sc_rev_04`

- **Dimension:** `securityCompetition`
- **Text:** Democracies with settled borders can treat one another as lasting security partners.
- **Advocate:** Bruce Russett provides classic evidence that established democracies rarely fight each other.
- **Misread risk:** The wording may capture beliefs about democracy itself and blur the security-competition signal.

### SELECTED — `v21_sc_rev_05`

- **Dimension:** `securityCompetition`
- **Text:** Deep regional cooperation can make armed conflict among member states politically unthinkable.
- **Advocate:** Karl Deutsch defined security communities as relationships where member states expect peaceful change.
- **Misread risk:** Respondents may think only of the European Union, narrowing a general claim to one regional case.

**Selection rationale:** `v21_sc_rev_01` and `v21_sc_rev_02` test durable restraint and credible intentions directly. `v21_sc_rev_05` adds the distinct security-community claim that repeated regional cooperation can change expectations about the use of force.

## Institutions

### Candidate — `v21_in_rev_01`

- **Dimension:** `institutions`
- **Text:** States follow international rules chiefly when compliance serves their immediate national interests.
- **Advocate:** John Mearsheimer argues that institutions exert little independent effect when power and security incentives point elsewhere.
- **Misread risk:** The claim may reward a generally cynical view of compliance and collapse legal obligation into short-term calculation.

### SELECTED — `v21_in_rev_03`

- **Dimension:** `institutions`
- **Text:** International agreements endure when participating states retain enough power to enforce the bargain.
- **Advocate:** Stephen Krasner shows how international rules work within bargains created by state power and interest.
- **Misread risk:** Respondents may treat “enforce” as military coercion, although monitoring and retaliation also count.

### Candidate — `v21_in_rev_02`

- **Dimension:** `institutions`
- **Text:** Great powers can set aside institutional rulings when vital security interests are at stake.
- **Advocate:** Kenneth Waltz argues that survival pressures constrain great powers more strongly than institutional decisions.
- **Misread risk:** “Vital interests” can become a catch-all and may invite respondents to imagine only existential crises.

### Candidate — `v21_in_rev_04`

- **Dimension:** `institutions`
- **Text:** Informal bargaining among major powers often produces durable cooperation during periods of rivalry.
- **Advocate:** Henry Kissinger presents stable order as the product of major powers accepting and bargaining within a shared settlement.
- **Misread risk:** Some may read informal bargaining as secret diplomacy, producing a reaction about transparency.

### Candidate — `v21_in_rev_05`

- **Dimension:** `institutions`
- **Text:** National vetoes protect the consent that gives international cooperation its political legitimacy.
- **Advocate:** John Bolton argues that international authority requires explicit sovereign consent and national constitutional control.
- **Misread risk:** “Vetoes” may trigger views about the UN Security Council instead of consent in general.

**Selection rationale:** `v21_in_rev_03` supplies the one additional reverse-coded institutions item needed for the coverage floor. It offers a power-based account of institutional durability without making short-term self-interest the only reason states comply.

## Domestic filters

### SELECTED — `v21_df_rev_01`

- **Dimension:** `domesticFilters`
- **Text:** States facing the same external threat usually adopt similar policies despite different political systems.
- **Advocate:** Kenneth Waltz argues that competitive pressures push differently governed states toward similar strategies.
- **Misread risk:** “Same threat” may feel unrealistic because states rarely perceive threats identically.

### SELECTED — `v21_df_rev_04`

- **Dimension:** `domesticFilters`
- **Text:** Regime change rarely alters a state's basic interests when its external position stays constant.
- **Advocate:** John Mearsheimer treats survival and relative power as enduring interests across regime types.
- **Misread risk:** “Regime change” may evoke revolutions or coups, where disruption itself changes the state's external position.

### Candidate — `v21_df_rev_02`

- **Dimension:** `domesticFilters`
- **Text:** Geography and military capability set the main limits on a state's foreign policy.
- **Advocate:** Nicholas Spykman identifies geography as foreign policy's most permanent constraint.
- **Misread risk:** The wording may pull strongly toward geography and understate economic constraints.

### Candidate — `v21_df_rev_03`

- **Dimension:** `domesticFilters`
- **Text:** Leaders usually preserve core foreign policies because abrupt change carries high strategic costs.
- **Advocate:** Stephen Walt explains policy continuity through persistent external threats, alliances, and the costs of realignment.
- **Misread risk:** The item could capture status quo bias or bureaucratic inertia instead of external constraint.

### Candidate — `v21_df_rev_05`

- **Dimension:** `domesticFilters`
- **Text:** Public opinion has little lasting effect on foreign policy during prolonged security competition.
- **Advocate:** Walter Lippmann argued that mass opinion on foreign affairs is volatile and often requires expert judgment.
- **Misread risk:** The wording may provoke a reaction about democratic values because “little lasting effect” can sound dismissive.

**Selection rationale:** `v21_df_rev_01` and `v21_df_rev_04` directly test whether external position outweighs domestic difference across governments and political transitions.

## Norms and identity

### SELECTED — `v21_ni_rev_01`

- **Dimension:** `normsIdentity`
- **Text:** States define their interests mainly through security needs, resources, and geographic position.
- **Advocate:** Kenneth Waltz derives state interests from survival and capabilities under external competition.
- **Misread risk:** “Resources” may be read as a political-economy claim and create overlap with that dimension.

### SELECTED — `v21_ni_rev_05`

- **Dimension:** `normsIdentity`
- **Text:** Recognition from other states rarely changes what governments seek in major security disputes.
- **Advocate:** Hans Morgenthau defines national interest through power, giving recognition limited independent force.
- **Misread risk:** “Recognition” may be read narrowly as formal diplomatic recognition instead of standing or status.

### Candidate — `v21_ni_rev_02`

- **Dimension:** `normsIdentity`
- **Text:** Material incentives usually explain foreign-policy change even when official narratives remain stable.
- **Advocate:** Robert Gilpin explains major policy change through shifts in power and wealth.
- **Misread risk:** Respondents may read the item as a claim about propaganda instead of a claim about how interests form.

### Candidate — `v21_ni_rev_03`

- **Dimension:** `normsIdentity`
- **Text:** Status claims become politically important when they affect concrete power, access, or security.
- **Advocate:** Robert Gilpin connects prestige and status to control over international order and the distribution of benefits.
- **Misread risk:** Respondents may agree only when status claims bring concrete concessions, leaving their view of status itself unclear.

### Candidate — `v21_ni_rev_04`

- **Dimension:** `normsIdentity`
- **Text:** Shared values hold alliances together while members continue to face a common threat.
- **Advocate:** Stephen Walt argues that alliances form and persist mainly in response to common threats.
- **Misread risk:** The common-threat clause may make this item measure security competition as strongly as identity.

**Selection rationale:** `v21_ni_rev_01` and `v21_ni_rev_05` most cleanly test whether interests and recognition have independent social content beyond material position.

## Political economy

### SELECTED — `v21_pe_rev_02`

- **Dimension:** `politicalEconomy`
- **Text:** Open markets usually give states enough flexibility to replace suppliers and absorb economic pressure.
- **Advocate:** Jagdish Bhagwati defends open trade as a source of adjustment, competition, and prosperity under pressure.
- **Misread risk:** “Enough flexibility” varies with market size, so small-state respondents may reject the claim based on national capacity.

### Candidate — `v21_pe_rev_03`

- **Dimension:** `politicalEconomy`
- **Text:** Sound domestic policy gives states substantial protection from international financial shocks.
- **Advocate:** Stanley Fischer emphasizes sound domestic financial systems and macroeconomic policy as buffers against external shocks.
- **Misread risk:** The item may sound like blame directed at crisis-hit states or an endorsement of one economic policy package.

### Candidate — `v21_pe_rev_01`

- **Dimension:** `politicalEconomy`
- **Text:** Military power and alliance commitments usually decide crises before economic dependence becomes decisive.
- **Advocate:** John Mearsheimer argues that security competition can override the gains and dependencies created by commerce.
- **Misread risk:** The item may measure security competition and prompt recall of a recent war.

### Candidate — `v21_pe_rev_04`

- **Dimension:** `politicalEconomy`
- **Text:** Market competition usually limits any state's ability to turn economic leadership into lasting political control.
- **Advocate:** Friedrich Hayek presents competition as a discovery process that disperses economic power and frustrates central direction.
- **Misread risk:** Respondents may answer from domestic views about business regulation instead of international economic leverage.

### Candidate — `v21_pe_rev_05`

- **Dimension:** `politicalEconomy`
- **Text:** Economic sanctions rarely alter state policy unless leaders already face severe military pressure.
- **Advocate:** Robert Pape finds that sanctions rarely coerce states when military pressure or concessions explain apparent successes.
- **Misread risk:** Respondents may treat military pressure as a policy endorsement, although the item asks about causal effectiveness.

**Selection rationale:** `v21_pe_rev_02` supplies the one additional reverse-coded political-economy item needed for the coverage floor. It directly contests structural vulnerability through market adjustment without turning the item into a judgment about crisis-hit states' domestic policy.

## Restraint

### SELECTED — `v21_rs_rev_04`

- **Dimension:** `restraint`
- **Text:** Preventing a hostile power from dominating a key region can justify long-term commitments far from home.
- **Advocate:** Zbigniew Brzezinski advocated sustained commitments to prevent hostile domination of Eurasia's key regions.
- **Misread risk:** “Key region” may depend heavily on a respondent's own national perspective.

### SELECTED — `v21_rs_rev_05`

- **Dimension:** `restraint`
- **Text:** Sustained forward presence gives allies confidence and denies rivals room to test regional limits.
- **Advocate:** Robert Art's selective engagement uses forward power to defend vital regions and reassure allies.
- **Misread risk:** Some may equate forward presence with permanent basing and answer from local political concerns.

### Candidate — `v21_rs_rev_01`

- **Dimension:** `restraint`
- **Text:** A favorable balance of power is safest when states convert temporary advantages into durable strategic gains.
- **Advocate:** Hal Brands argues that durable order depends on preserving favorable power and strategic primacy.
- **Misread risk:** “Temporary advantages” may sound opportunistic and attract moral reactions unrelated to strategic restraint.

### Candidate — `v21_rs_rev_02`

- **Dimension:** `restraint`
- **Text:** Credible alliances require leading states to accept substantial costs and risks beyond immediate self-defense.
- **Advocate:** Robert Kagan argues that alliance systems and international order require leading powers to bear sustained costs.
- **Misread risk:** The item may be read as a narrow alliance burden-sharing question.

### Candidate — `v21_rs_rev_03`

- **Dimension:** `restraint`
- **Text:** Early military pressure can prevent larger wars by convincing challengers that expansion will fail.
- **Advocate:** Paul Nitze treated visible strength and firm pressure as foundations for deterrence.
- **Misread risk:** Respondents may picture preventive war, although “pressure” also covers deployments and other coercive signals.

**Selection rationale:** `v21_rs_rev_04` and `v21_rs_rev_05` provide bounded, serious arguments for extended engagement without making opportunism or preventive war the central test.

## Order and justice

### Candidate — `v21_oj_rev_01`

- **Dimension:** `orderJustice`
- **Text:** Governments that systematically terrorize their citizens weaken their claim to sovereign protection.
- **Advocate:** Kofi Annan argues that sovereignty carries responsibility and loses force when governments abuse their citizens.
- **Misread risk:** “Terrorize” evokes extreme cases and may produce near-universal agreement.

### SELECTED — `v21_oj_rev_02`

- **Dimension:** `orderJustice`
- **Text:** International courts should pursue grave crimes even when prosecutions complicate peace negotiations.
- **Advocate:** Luis Moreno Ocampo, the International Criminal Court's founding prosecutor, defends prosecution as a path to peace and legal order.
- **Misread risk:** Respondents may answer from their trust in international courts instead of the justice-order tradeoff.

### Candidate — `v21_oj_rev_03`

- **Dimension:** `orderJustice`
- **Text:** Peace settlements should preserve a credible path to accountability for grave wartime crimes.
- **Advocate:** Kathryn Sikkink documents the spread of human-rights prosecutions and argues that accountability can deter future abuse and support stability.
- **Misread risk:** “Credible path” may sound like an easy compromise and attract agreement across opposing positions.

### Candidate — `v21_oj_rev_04`

- **Dimension:** `orderJustice`
- **Text:** Regional organizations should be able to authorize limited civilian protection when global bodies remain blocked.
- **Advocate:** The African Union's Constitutive Act claims a regional right to intervene in cases of grave crimes.
- **Misread risk:** Respondents may read “authorize” as settled international law, although the principle remains politically contested.

### Candidate — `v21_oj_rev_05`

- **Dimension:** `orderJustice`
- **Text:** Democratic governments should accept real strategic costs to defend basic rights beyond their borders.
- **Advocate:** Václav Havel placed human rights and moral responsibility at the center of democratic foreign policy.
- **Misread risk:** “Democratic governments” may imply democratic exceptionalism and blur the universal rights principle.

**Selection rationale:** `v21_oj_rev_02` supplies the one additional reverse-coded order-and-justice item needed for the coverage floor. It tests criminal accountability while keeping the cost to peace negotiations explicit and avoids the near-universal extreme embedded in `v21_oj_rev_01`.

## Forward-coded Likert rewrite slate

The audit covered all 19 previous forward-coded Likert items. The four entries below are prompt-only rewrites. Their IDs, dimensions, scoring directions, kinds, modes, clarifications, and other metadata remain unchanged.

### REWRITE — `sc2`

- **Preserved:** ID `sc2`; dimension `securityCompetition`; forward-coded direction (`reverse: false`); kind `likert`; modes `standard` and `analyst`; existing clarification and all other metadata.
- **Current text:** States often prepare for danger because they cannot be sure others will stay benign.
- **Replacement text:** Uncertainty about another state's long-term intentions makes continued military preparation necessary even during sustained cooperation.
- **Reason:** The current wording makes agreement easy: uncertainty exists in almost any relationship, and “prepare for danger” can mean ordinary prudence. The replacement asks respondents to endorse the stronger claim that uncertainty makes continued military preparation necessary despite sustained cooperation.
- **Misread risk:** “Military preparation” may still be read as routine contingency planning instead of competitive force development.

### REWRITE — `df1`

- **Preserved:** ID `df1`; dimension `domesticFilters`; forward-coded direction (`reverse: false`); kind `likert`; modes `standard` and `analyst`; existing clarification and all other metadata.
- **Current text:** Changes in who governs and whom they answer to often shift foreign policy as much as outside threats do.
- **Replacement text:** A new governing coalition often redirects foreign policy even when the external threat remains unchanged.
- **Reason:** The current prompt combines leadership turnover, political accountability, and a comparison with external threats. The replacement isolates one testable claim and raises the threshold above the broad observation that domestic politics sometimes matters.
- **Misread risk:** “Governing coalition” may sound specific to parliamentary systems, although coalitions of political support also exist in presidential and less democratic systems.

### REWRITE — `ni2`

- **Preserved:** ID `ni2`; dimension `normsIdentity`; forward-coded direction (`reverse: false`); kind `likert`; modes `standard` and `analyst`; existing clarification and all other metadata.
- **Current text:** Status, recognition, and legitimacy help shape what states want, not just how they pursue fixed interests.
- **Replacement text:** A state's demand for recognition often changes which foreign-policy goals its leaders consider important.
- **Reason:** The current prompt stacks three abstract concepts and uses a polished contrast with “fixed interests,” allowing respondents to agree with only part of the claim. The replacement uses one concept and states the proposed effect on interests directly.
- **Misread risk:** Respondents may treat a demand for recognition as a leader's search for prestige instead of a socially shaped state interest.

### REWRITE — `an_pe4`

- **Preserved:** ID `an_pe4`; dimension `politicalEconomy`; forward-coded direction (`reverse: false`); kind `likert`; mode `analyst`; existing clarification and all other metadata.
- **Current text:** In the coming decade, power will depend as much on who sets the rules and controls the infrastructure for chips, cloud services, payments, and cross-border data as on military strength or territory.
- **Replacement text:** Control over technology rules and critical infrastructure will rival military strength as a source of international power in the coming decade.
- **Reason:** The current 32-word prompt combines rule-setting, four technology domains, military strength, territory, and a forecast. The replacement preserves the substantive comparison while reducing the item to one claim within the 12–24-word target.
- **Misread risk:** “Technology rules and critical infrastructure” may prompt respondents to answer from current AI or semiconductor debates instead of their broader view of international power.
