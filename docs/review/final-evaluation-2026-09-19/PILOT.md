# A small pilot that can change the product

Do the short trust pass first, then test with people. The purpose is to learn whether the experience is understandable, fair to competing views and useful for discussion. Do not ask the first pilot to establish psychometric validity or a business model.

## Before invitations

Fix the older Foundation history's treatment of shared links and its false saved status. Correct AI review's absolute “No data leaves your browser” promise and the case country count. Make the neutral tie and Perspective movement/stability claims accurate, or keep those surfaces out of the first unmoderated assignment. Ensure the chosen feedback channel works; the live beta page currently has no booking destination.

Keep one dated release for the pilot. Record wording/scorer versions privately in the evaluation notes without collecting participants' result URLs. Explain that the tool provides authored comparisons and is being evaluated. A person may skip any political question or stop. Ask separately before recording a session. This is a product usability pilot; a formal research study should have its own appropriate review, consent and data plan.

## First round: six to eight people

Recruit a mix of readers: several with limited IR knowledge, several who study or work with these ideas, and one or two who can assess the Chinese experience if that audience matters now. This is purposive recruitment for finding problems, not a representative sample. Invite candid criticism explicitly. Do not fill the group only with people likely to praise the creator.

Run individual sessions of roughly 35–45 minutes. Do not make everyone complete every optional questionnaire. Assign one main journey and a shorter exercise. Divide routes across the group so each optional module gets observed. Give at least one participant a shared result as a recipient, and one a returning draft.

Suggested invitation wording:

> I’m testing whether this interactive helps people explain a political judgment and consider a serious alternative. I’d like you to use it while describing what you think it means. I’m testing the product, not your knowledge. It’s useful to tell me when the options miss your view or the result says something you did not mean.

## Tasks and observations

| Task | Ask the participant to do | Observe without teaching them |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival | Explain what the site offers and choose a starting point. | Can they say what they will get? Do they understand the distinction between answering and browsing? |
| Questionnaire | Complete Foundation or the assigned module, thinking aloud on a few selected questions. | Terms they cannot paraphrase; compatible statements they treat as opposites; choices that bundle a cause with a policy; pressure to pick the “reasonable” answer. |
| Review | Find and change one answer, then generate. | Whether they know nothing has been finalized before generation; navigation friction; whether edits retain the intended answers. |
| Result comprehension | Explain the result in their own words without reading it aloud. | Do they name a concrete tradeoff, or only repeat the archetype? Do they think the score is a percentile, probability, intelligence measure or fixed identity? |
| Evidence check | Find what supports one statement in the result. | Can they trace it to an actual answer, a coded model comparison or an illustrative example? Do they notice the difference? |
| Disagreement | Identify one part of the interpretation that may be wrong. | Whether the product lets them explain the disagreement, and whether it overstates what their answers imply. |
| Rival argument | Explain the strongest alternative and what evidence would change their view. | Is the alternative a serious argument rather than a straw man? Does it change the quality of the discussion? |
| Sharing | Preview a link/share action or open a synthetic supplied result. | Whose result they think it is, what they think the URL contains, and whether they expect it to be saved as their own. No need to transmit a personal result. |
| Return | Leave and resume a draft or revisit Profile. | Whether the saved state is predictable and whether the next action is obvious. |
| Decision exercise | Complete Access or Verification, including the changed condition. | Whether they understand what changed and what stayed fixed; whether their stated reason fits the interpretation; whether withholding judgment is respected. |
| Optional Future/role | One assigned Futures or Perspective journey. | Whether uncertainty remains uncertainty, and whether modeled offsets are mistaken for observed changes in the person's worldview. |

Avoid leading questions such as “Was the result accurate?” Ask instead: “Which sentence is best supported by what you answered?” and “Which sentence goes beyond what you meant?” Do not explain Musyawarah, the meter calibration or the family model until after recording what the participant thought. The explanation can come afterward as part of a respectful debrief.

## A minimal observation sheet

Use session labels such as P01, not unnecessary identity details. Record:

- assigned route and release date;
- task completed independently / completed with help / stopped;
- confusing public phrase or question identifier;
- participant's explanation of the confusion, paraphrased with permission;
- mistaken inference about the result;
- one concrete useful insight, if any;
- whether the participant chose a next activity without prompting;
- proposed change and which evidence would justify it.

Do not use the app's general corrections email to collect quiz answers or Profile URLs. The current policy explicitly excludes those. If the project later needs response-level research data, design that collection separately instead of quietly repurposing this usability pilot.

## Gates after the first round

These are practical release decisions, not statistical estimates:

1. **Comprehension:** any recurring confusion about what is being measured, whose result is displayed, or what a number means triggers a revision before broader invitations.
2. **Fairness:** if multiple participants cannot represent a common, coherent position with the offered answers, review that item. Do not solve it only by adding a disclaimer after scoring.
3. **Payoff:** if people can repeat the label but cannot explain one tradeoff or rival argument, revise the result's first screen and example before adding content.
4. **Trust:** any reproduced ownership, persistence, link-disclosure or wrong-claim defect blocks the affected sharing path until corrected.
5. **Utility:** if people report only that it is “interesting,” seek a concrete use before inferring demand. A better discussion, a useful classroom exchange or a voluntary second exercise is stronger evidence.

After the changes, observe a few new readers on the revised flow. Familiar participants can confirm a fix but cannot reveal the same first-use confusion reliably.

## Separate expert review

Give two or three independent IR/methods readers a bounded brief: review the seven Core pairs, the mapping from answers to claims, the four authored family profiles, and one result near a boundary. Ask them to distinguish factual errors, theoretical disagreement, missing traditions and unsupported inference. Do not request a vague blessing of the entire product.

For a teaching pilot, ask an instructor to run one decision exercise without the creator facilitating. Ask afterward whether they would use it again, in what class, and what preparation it required. A repeat request is a more informative commercial signal than praise for the interface.

The next development decision should follow those observations. If the strongest value is discussion, invest in clearer cases and facilitation. If users chiefly want self-location, investigate the measurement problems deliberately. If neither produces concrete value, narrow the promise before building more.
