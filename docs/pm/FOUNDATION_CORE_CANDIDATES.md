# Foundation’s fourteen questions

English wording candidate for discussion, 26 November 2026. The **Current** passages below reproduce the fourteen administered statements in screen order. Each **Candidate** uses the existing seven-point agreement scale. The proposition and change notes are for the editors. Only the candidate statement and any agreed, collapsed definition belong on the question screen.

**Decision in brief.** Eleven candidates aim to preserve what readers are judging. Items 7, 11 and 14 change the judgment enough to need a method decision. Start the wording pilot with items 2 and 5, where shorter phrasing can be tested without changing what counts as agreement. Test 7, 11 and 14 separately. No candidate in this document has been approved for administration. “Equivalent” is an editorial hypothesis. The judgment covers the proposition, who acts, the circumstances, the strength of the statement and what answering asks the reader to do. The notes identify the important residual risk for each. Read the candidates with the adjacent item in mind. Two agreements can make sense even where the current score combines them in opposite directions.

## 1. Military preparation

**Current** · Uncertainty about another state's long-term intentions makes continued military preparation necessary even during sustained cooperation.

**Candidate** · Two countries have cooperated for years. Uncertainty about the other’s long-term intentions still makes it necessary for each to keep its military ready.

**Proposition** · Does uncertainty make continued readiness necessary even after a long period of cooperation?

**Change** · Equivalent. Two countries, sustained cooperation, uncertainty and the necessity judgment remain. The first sentence sets the condition for the second. Check whether “keep its military ready” captures continuing preparation. Some readers may think only of existing forces.

**Optional help** · None.

Item `sc2`.

## 2. A rival’s intentions

**Current** · A rival's sustained restraint can provide credible evidence that its peaceful intentions will last.

**Candidate** · Suppose a rival has held back for a long time. That can be good evidence that its peaceful intentions will last.

**Proposition** · Can a long record of restraint give another country reason to believe its rival will continue to seek peace?

**Change** · Equivalent. The observer judges the strength of evidence. “Can” leaves room for exceptions. The question makes no demand to reduce readiness. Check what action readers imagined when the rival “held back.”

**Optional help** · None.

Item `v21_sc_rev_02`.

## 3. Keeping an agreement in force

**Current** · Monitoring and repeated contact can keep international agreements alive even when trust is thin.

**Candidate** · Countries that barely trust each other can keep an agreement going by staying in touch and checking whether each side keeps its promises.

**Proposition** · Can monitoring and repeated contact help participating countries keep an agreement going despite low trust?

**Change** · Equivalent in intent. Two mechanisms remain in the same claim. “Staying in touch” leaves the form of contact open. Check that readers judge whether the agreement lasts. A future attempt to measure monitoring separately would need another item.

**Optional help** · None.

Item `in2`.

## 4. Enforcing an agreement

**Current** · International agreements endure when participating states retain enough power to enforce the bargain.

**Candidate** · International agreements last when the countries taking part keep enough power to enforce the terms.

**Proposition** · Does the participants’ continued ability to enforce terms help account for an agreement’s survival?

**Change** · Equivalent. The participants, their continuing power and the agreement’s survival stay in view. “When” leaves room for other causes. Adding “only” would turn enforcement into a necessary condition.

**Optional help** · Enforcing the terms means taking action when a participant breaks the agreement.

Item `v21_in_rev_03`.

## 5. A change of government

**Current** · A new governing coalition often redirects foreign policy even when the external threat remains unchanged.

**Candidate** · A new governing coalition often changes the direction of a country’s foreign policy even when it faces the same threat from abroad.

**Proposition** · Can a change in who governs often redirect policy while the external threat holds steady?

**Change** · Equivalent. The actor remains a coalition. “Direction” retains the distinction between a policy shift and a minor adjustment. Keep the constant-threat condition from the approved English clarification. A named country would introduce additional assumptions.

**Optional help** · A governing coalition is a group of political parties or factions that govern together.

Item `df1`.

## 6. Interests after regime change

**Current** · Regime change rarely alters a state's basic interests when its external position stays constant.

**Candidate** · A change in a country’s political system rarely changes its basic interests if its position in the world stays the same.

**Proposition** · If the external position holds steady, do basic interests usually survive a change of regime?

**Change** · Equivalent in intent, with “regime” read as a system of rule. The outcome is basic interests. Item 5 asks about policy direction after a change in the groups governing. Agreement with both has an intelligible meaning.

**Optional help** · Basic interests are the enduring goals a country’s leaders see as important. Ask in testing whether “position in the world” needs a narrower definition.

Item `v21_df_rev_04`.

## 7. Seeking recognition

**Current** · A state's demand for recognition often changes which foreign-policy goals its leaders consider important.

**Candidate** · When a country seeks international recognition of its status, this often changes which foreign-policy goals its leaders consider important.

**Proposition** · Does the pursuit of international standing influence the goals leaders value?

**Change** · Substantive. The revision selects recognition of status over formal recognition of statehood. It also softens a “demand” into seeking recognition. The outcome remains the importance leaders attach to goals. Test both senses before narrowing the term.

**Optional help** · International status means a country’s standing among other countries. Withhold this definition until the intended sense of recognition is agreed.

Item `ni2`.

## 8. Recognition in a security dispute

**Current** · Recognition from other states rarely changes what governments seek in major security disputes.

**Candidate** · In major security disputes, recognition from other countries rarely changes what a government wants.

**Proposition** · Does recognition seldom change the ends a government seeks in a major dispute over security?

**Change** · Equivalent. The scope remains a major security dispute, narrower than the foreign-policy setting in item 7. Preserve “rarely.” Adding the proposed status-only definition from item 7 would turn this into a separate substantive decision.

**Optional help** · Retain the broad term for this candidate. Ask whether “recognition” meant status, acceptance of a claim, or formal diplomatic recognition before selecting a definition.

Item `v21_ni_rev_05`.

## 9. Who benefits from economic rules?

**Current** · Economic rules often give stronger states lasting structural advantages that weaker states must work around.

**Candidate** · Economic rules often give stronger countries lasting advantages that weaker countries have to work around.

**Proposition** · Do the rules themselves repeatedly put stronger countries in a better position?

**Change** · Equivalent. “Economic rules … give” locates the advantage in the rules. “Lasting” preserves the time horizon. Agreement says nothing about which remedy, if any, the reader favors.

**Optional help** · None.

Item `pe2`.

## 10. Replacing suppliers

**Current** · Open markets usually give states enough flexibility to replace suppliers and absorb economic pressure.

**Candidate** · Open markets usually let countries switch suppliers and withstand economic pressure.

**Proposition** · Do open markets usually offer enough room to change suppliers and absorb pressure?

**Change** · Equivalent in intent. Keep both substitution and resilience. Disagreement cannot show which part the reader doubts. A deadline, a scarce component or a question about who should subsidize alternatives would change the scope or task.

**Optional help** · In an open market, goods or services can be bought and sold across borders with few restrictions.

Item `v21_pe_rev_02`.

## 11. Taking on another commitment

**Current** · Avoiding overextension is usually more important than pressing every opening for lasting advantage.

**Candidate** · A new overseas commitment could bring lasting gains. A country should usually decline if accepting would leave fewer resources for its existing commitments.

**Proposition** · How readily should a government forgo a plausible gain to protect its ability to meet commitments it already has?

**Change** · Substantive. One plausible opportunity replaces “every opening.” Fewer resources for existing commitments gives readers a prospective cost. “Overextension” already judges the burden excessive. Overseas commitments narrow the range of strategies. The reader now weighs one decision under stated conditions. Choose which scope and degree of strain to measure before scoring it.

**Optional help** · None.

Item `rs2`.

## 12. Preventing regional dominance

**Current** · Preventing a hostile power from dominating a key region can justify long-term commitments far from home.

**Candidate** · A country can have good reason to make long-term commitments far from home to keep a hostile power from dominating an important region.

**Proposition** · Can preventing regional domination justify a distant, lasting commitment?

**Change** · Equivalent. Both versions ask whether preventing domination can supply a sufficient reason. It expresses neither a general duty to intervene nor support for war. A country can accept this exception while usually favoring limits in item 11.

**Optional help** · None. Leave the means open.

Item `v21_rs_rev_04`.

## 13. Preserving order

**Current** · A stable international order is often worth preserving even when serious injustice remains unresolved.

**Candidate** · It is often worth preserving a stable international order even when serious injustices remain.

**Proposition** · Does maintaining a stable order often merit the cost of leaving serious injustice unresolved?

**Change** · Equivalent. Keep “often,” the normative judgment and the unresolved harm. Specifying a single victim, injustice or institution would narrow the question. During testing, distinguish objection to the harm from doubt that such an order can stay stable.

**Optional help** · International order refers to the arrangements that govern relations among states.

Item `oj1`.

## 14. Outside intervention

**Current** · When mass atrocities become severe enough, outside powers can justifiably override a state's sovereignty to stop them.

**Candidate** · When mass atrocities become severe enough, other countries can be justified in using military force to stop them without the government’s consent.

**Proposition** · Can a sufficiently severe atrocity justify an exception to the state’s authority over action on its territory?

**Change** · Substantive. The current stem leaves the means open while its clarification specifies military action. Moving force and lack of consent into the stem makes every reader judge that narrower operation. Keep the high threshold and the judgment about justification. A follow-up about likely success would ask a different question.

**Optional help** · Mass atrocities are large-scale acts of extreme violence against civilians. Ask readers what would cross their threshold before offering any example.

Item `oj2`.

## What two answers can establish

A fictional reader gives item 9 a 7 and item 10 a 2. They strongly agree that rules can advantage stronger states and disagree that open markets usually let countries replace suppliers and absorb pressure. That is evidence about their view of how markets work. The two answers contain no choice about tariffs, subsidies or who should control investment. If the system has only a dimension score, describe the model’s association with the tradition. A shared score cannot establish those two individual answers.

The same reader gives items 11 and 12 a 6 each. They agree both that overextension is a danger and that preventing regional dominance can justify distant commitments. The two responses yield a midpoint on the restraint scale. With their own saved answers available, a reader can see exactly which statements produced it. A midpoint alone also fits two neutral responses. It cannot establish dual commitments or a new identity.

## The extended ceasefire item

The ceasefire scenario is outside the fourteen. The existing prompt asks, “A brutal war may end only if outside powers delay accountability and accept an unequal settlement. What is the deeper issue?” Its choices mix an immediate priority, an expectation about deterrence, a settlement design and an explanation of bargaining power. “The strongest answer” in one choice can lead readers. The order-and-justice contribution is 3.1 for stopping the harm and 6.2 for holding the accountability line. The high end of this scale generally favors order. The courts item about pursuing grave crimes despite costs to peace negotiations is reverse-coded. Editors need to say which meaning of order the ceasefire answers are meant to express. [Bank, choices and signals](../../content/instrument/foundation.v2.json) · [Current method disclosure](../../app/method/page.tsx)

**Candidate for an unscored priority question.** “Mediators expect a monitored ceasefire to reduce civilian deaths. One side will sign only if prosecutions are postponed for two years. Should the mediators support that deal?” Offer “Support the delay,” “Insist that prosecutions can proceed,” and “I need another fact to decide.” Ask anyone who wants to explain their choice which fact would change it. Neither immediate protection nor future accountability is guaranteed. A reader may want both and still judge the terms.

**Candidate for an unscored explanation question.** “The two sides sign after negotiators agree to postpone prosecutions. Which fact would help you explain why they signed?” Test responses such as “Which side could afford to keep fighting?”, “Who could verify a breach of the ceasefire?” and “What assurances did the negotiators give about later prosecutions?” These choices identify evidence a reader would seek. They do not rank how just the settlement is. Do not assign existing option scores to either candidate. Choose the primary task before refining options or testing agreement between reviewers.

## A small decision exercise for editors

1. Give an IR reader and a reader new to the subject the **current** or **candidate** statement first, alternating versions across interviews. Before opening help, ask them to explain who acts, what changes and why they chose that point on the scale. Hear the paraphrase before supplying a country, theory or example.
2. Probe only the vulnerable distinctions. For item 2, ask what “will last” means and whether credible intentions can coexist with continued military preparation. For item 5, ask what stayed the same. For 7 and 8, ask who grants recognition and what the government wanted before and afterward. For 11 and 12, ask which commitment they imagined and what it would displace. For 13 and 14, ask whether they judged justification, likely success or legal authorization.
3. Open any proposed definition, ask for a second paraphrase, and record whether it changed the intended comparison or suggested an answer. Compare old and new responses only after the unaided discussion. A changed number can reflect a real change of task, uncertainty or reflection. Preserve the reason. Treat these interviews as comprehension evidence, without estimating score equivalence or the prevalence of a difficulty.
4. Approve individual wording changes with their definitions as a new English copy revision. The supported English revisions are 1 and 2. Revision 2 changes the **df1 clarification**, while the issued stem remains identical. Register the new revision, render by the administered copy revision, retain its provenance for drafts and answer evidence, and replay both old and new links. Chinese has its own revisions, currently through 3, and needs separate adaptation.
