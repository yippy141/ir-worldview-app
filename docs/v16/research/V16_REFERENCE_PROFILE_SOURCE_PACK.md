# Source Pack for the IR Worldview Inventory Reference Profile Layer

## Methodology note

This pack is designed as a first-pass anchor set for an evidence-coded Reference Profile layer. Because you specified a twelve-profile cap, I selected four canonical IR thinkers, four live geopolitical currents, and four AI-governance currents that give wide spread across the seven foundation dimensions while remaining usable as comparative reference points inside the application. The seven coding keys and their fixed order in the public repository are: `securityCompetition`, `institutions`, `domesticFilters`, `normsIdentity`, `politicalEconomy`, `restraint`, and `orderJustice`, corresponding to the labels you supplied. The same public repo also exposes a related AI-governance layer with archetypes that overlap with several of the currents below, which helped in naming and harmonising the AI section. citeturn7view0turn7view1turn7view2turn6view0turn6view3

The coding rule used here is narrow. I coded only public writings, official statements, legislative or executive actions, and verifiable institutional behaviour. I did not infer private motives. For living political currents, the recent evidence window is weighted toward January 2025 to July 12, 2026, with earlier material included only where it plainly defines the current. For thinkers, the window is broader and anchored to canonical texts, with later writings added only where they materially alter coding. For AI-governance currents, the window is 2023 to July 12, 2026, with special weight on 2025–2026 documents because the discourse has shifted quickly. citeturn17search6turn19search0turn20view0turn28view2turn27view2

A “strong” dimension means the public record is ample enough to support both a directional judgement and a 1–7 estimate. “Partial” means the direction is visible but the boundary is blurrier. “Sparse” means there is suggestive evidence, but the case is too thin for confidence. “Uncoded” means I would not use the dimension operationally yet. I recommend a numeric estimate only where the evidence supports it, and every strong estimate below is backed by at least two independent sources. Where the public record contains tension or movement over time, I note it explicitly rather than forcing a false purity. citeturn32search0turn35view0turn37view1turn38view1turn24news30

One practical caveat is worth stating. In the public repository pages I could access, I found the dimension contract and AI-layer labels, but not a dedicated public JSON schema for evidence-coded reference profiles. The appendix therefore uses an inferred schema aligned to the published dimension keys and to your requested fields. citeturn7view0turn7view1turn7view2turn6view0turn6view1

### John Mearsheimer

**Scope and evidence window** — Person; associated mainly with offensive realism, with later policy work often attached to offshore balancing. Evidence window: 1994–2021 for theory, with 2016–2019 restraint writings used to capture later strategic prescriptions. **As of** 2026-07-12. citeturn32search0turn31view1turn33view1turn33view3

**Security rivalry** — **Strong; estimate 7.** Mearsheimer’s core claim is that states in anarchy must compete for power because no higher authority can guarantee their survival. His later summary of realism keeps that logic intact even when he distinguishes realism from liberal crusading. citeturn32search0turn33view2

**Institutions and rules** — **Strong; estimate 1.** He has long argued that institutions have minimal independent effect on state behaviour, and his later writing on the liberal international order treats institutions as subordinate to power and nationalism. citeturn32search0turn31view1

**Domestic politics** — **Partial; estimate 2.** His baseline theory is systemic, not domestic. He has acknowledged that realism needs domestic-political help to explain some state behaviour, but that is an auxiliary move rather than the centre of gravity of his worldview. citeturn32search2turn33view2

**Identity and legitimacy** — **Partial; estimate 3.** Nationalism is important in his later work, especially as a constraint on liberal projects, but identity is not treated as the constitutive engine of state interests in the Wendtian sense. citeturn33view3turn31view1

**Markets and dependence** — **Partial; estimate 3.** He recognises open markets as part of order-building projects, yet tends to place security competition above economic interdependence as the decisive variable. citeturn31view1turn33view1

**Restraint and advantage** — **Partial; estimate 4.** His theory of offensive realism pushes toward advantage-seeking, but his policy writing on offshore balancing is distinctly restraining. The right coding is therefore middling, not maximalist. citeturn33view2turn33view1

**Order and justice** — **Strong; estimate 6.** He is consistently sceptical of remaking the world in the name of rights or democracy when those projects destabilise order and provoke backlash. The balance tips toward order, sovereignty and prudence. citeturn33view1turn31view1

**Contradictory evidence and change over time** — The key tension is between high-theory offensive realism and later offshore-balancing prescriptions. He did not become an institutionalist or a solidarist; he became more explicit about the costs of liberal hegemony and the strategic case for restraint. citeturn33view1turn33view2
