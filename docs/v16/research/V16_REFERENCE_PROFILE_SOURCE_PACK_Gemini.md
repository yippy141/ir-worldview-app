# **Evidence-Coded Reference Profiles for the IR Worldview Inventory**

## **1\. Methodology Note**

The ensuing report delineates a comprehensive reference layer for the International Relations (IR) Worldview Inventory. This analytical framework operates by translating the complex, often diffuse strategic postures of global actors into a standardized, machine-readable seven-dimension vector. The evidence window for all profiles extends strictly through July 12, 2026\. The architecture isolates public positions, codified doctrines, and systemic behavioral records, explicitly discarding conjecture regarding private motives, back-channel intentions, or unverified psychological drivers.  
To ensure high-fidelity mapping, the inventory evaluates entities across seven foundational IR dimensions: Security Rivalry, Institutions and Rules, Domestic Politics, Identity and Legitimacy, Markets and Dependence, Restraint and Advantage, and Order and Justice. Each dimension is assessed on a 1–7 scale. A score of 1 indicates total rejection or negligible salience of the dimension’s core premise, whereas a score of 7 reflects absolute centrality and maximal alignment.  
Estimates are provided exclusively when the evidentiary base—comprising primary policy documents, legislative actions, authorized speeches, and high-quality geopolitical reporting—is sufficiently robust. Dimensions lacking adequate documentation are explicitly marked as "Uncoded." Furthermore, the framework categorizes each subject by its taxonomic type: Individual Person, Political Movement, Codified Doctrine, or Analytical School. This structural distinction clarifies the scope of the analysis. Contradictory evidence, ideological fracturing, and chronological evolution are recorded directly within the profile justifications to preserve analytical integrity and highlight internal frictions within overarching geopolitical currents.

### **2.1. IR Thinkers and Analytical Schools**

The foundational theoretical frameworks of international relations provide the baseline vectors for the worldview inventory. These academic schools establish the conceptual vocabulary utilized by contemporary policymakers.

#### **John Mearsheimer (Offensive Realism)**

Offensive realism provides a strictly structural interpretation of global politics. The theory asserts that the anarchic nature of the international system forces great powers to maximize their relative capabilities relentlessly1. Survival represents the paramount objective, and it is achieved exclusively through regional hegemony3. The framework dismisses domestic political configurations, human nature, and international institutions as epiphenomenal, focusing entirely on the material distribution of power, primarily measured by military strength and latent economic capacity3.

#### **Robert Keohane (Neoliberal Institutionalism)**

Neoliberal institutionalism acknowledges the anarchic structure of the global system while demonstrating the viability of enduring cooperation. The framework focuses on how international regimes reduce transaction costs, provide vital information, and establish focal points for state coordination6. Complex interdependence alters state calculations, elevating economic and institutional linkages to a level where military force becomes a suboptimal tool for resolving disputes among integrated nations8.

#### **Alexander Wendt (Constructivism)**

Constructivism advances a structural idealist worldview, arguing that the international system is a social construction. Material structures—including military arsenals and economic resources—acquire geopolitical meaning exclusively through intersubjective interpretation12. The theory delineates how shared ideas constitute state identities and interests, manifesting in distinct cultures of anarchy (Hobbesian, Lockean, or Kantian)14.

#### **Susan Strange (Structural Power / IPE)**

Strange's framework of International Political Economy (IPE) fundamentally challenges state-centric realism by emphasizing structural power. This power dictates the frameworks within which states, multinational enterprises, and populations interact17. The system operates across four primary structures: security, production, finance, and knowledge. The diffusion of authority away from territorially defined governments has generated a chaotic environment of "ungovernance," elevating the role of markets and everyday mundane actions in shaping global outcomes18.
