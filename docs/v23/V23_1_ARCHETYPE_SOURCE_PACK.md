# Editorial and Evidence Pack for the IR Worldview Inventory

## Editorial memo

**Research baseline.** This review treats commit `f95cc7c1629e7e608910b2413b5d01bbf0fc974d` as frozen for the eight Foundation codes and names. At that commit, `content/archetypes.json` contains exactly the eight requested archetypes and already attaches the Melian Dialogue, *The Art of War*, Grotius, the Congress of Vienna, the Salt March, the ASEAN Way, the Monnet Plan, and Raúl Prebisch as historical analogues. Its analogue links are presently Wikipedia links. fileciteturn3file0L2-L2 The companion evidence file already recognizes several of the main problems identified below, particularly the extremity of Melos, the polysemy and modern use of 勢, the collision between Grotius and later “Grotian” terminology, the hierarchy of the Concert, controversies around Gandhi, the limits of ASEAN consensus, the mixed economy of the Monnet Plan, and the distinction between Prebisch and later dependencia. fileciteturn2file0L2-L2

The repository's own editorial standard is the right one for this exercise: fast comprehension, one idea per sentence, concrete language, no fake precision, and theory made legible without being flattened. fileciteturn4file0L2-L2 Its broader evidence contract is even more important here. Public claims should trace to source records; uncertainty should be stated plainly; source metadata and evidence class should be visible rather than hidden. fileciteturn5file0L2-L2

**Overall judgement.** All eight frozen names can remain usable as *editorial archetype names*, but none should be represented as a historically exact taxonomy. They work best as mnemonic comparisons between a pattern in the inventory and a body of historical argument. They work poorly when the analogy is turned into an identity claim. In particular, the product should never imply that Thucydides “was Kairos”, Sunzi “was Shi”, Grotius “was R+”, ASEAN “is Musyawarah”, or that any contemporary government or organisation belongs to one of the inventory's archetypes.

A useful common disclosure is:

> **Historical analogues are comparisons.** They illustrate a line of argument found in the result. They do not classify the person, state, movement, text, or institution named, and they do not imply endorsement of its wider record.

That sentence also solves an important methodological problem. The analogue may be strong on one dimension and weak on another. Melos strongly illustrates unequal power but only indirectly supports the product's emphasis on timing. The Monnet Plan strongly illustrates selective state coordination but weakly represents generic state ownership. Prebisch strongly supports centre-periphery analysis but is not interchangeable with later dependencia theory.

The research suggests the following editorial risk ranking:

The most important editorial intervention concerns **Grotian**. Grotius's *De Jure Belli ac Pacis* grounds obligation in natural law and the law of nations, and spends substantial effort identifying restraints that apply even in war. Later English School scholarship turned “Grotian” into a tradition centred on international society, law, institutions and, in some formulations, solidarism. Enforcement is relevant to parts of that tradition, but “law has force only when backed by credible enforcement” is not a safe statement of Grotius's own position. citeturn7search3turn7search11turn7search12turn6search3turn6search12

The second major intervention concerns **Musyawarah**. ASEAN's Charter does make consultation and consensus a basic decision-making principle, while also listing non-interference among ASEAN principles. Crucially, Article 20 does *not* make consensus an absolute dead end: when consensus cannot be achieved, the ASEAN Summit may determine how a particular decision is to be made. citeturn24view0turn24view1 Scholarship also shows that the “ASEAN Way” is contested in meaning and application, and that consensus can increase inclusion while imposing costs in speed, decisiveness and substantive ambition. citeturn10search15turn10search13turn10search18

The third intervention concerns **Kairos**. The Melian Dialogue is one of the clearest ancient texts for an argument that radically unequal capabilities shape what each side can demand. It is much less safe as a neutral emblem of prudent realism. The surrounding Thucydidean narrative, especially the subsequent Sicilian expedition, has repeatedly supported readings in which the Athenian argument illustrates the corruption and strategic blindness of imperial power. Contemporary classical scholarship likewise treats the episode as morally charged rather than a straightforward authorial endorsement of the Athenians. citeturn4search1turn4search7turn4search8 “Kairos”, meanwhile, is the inventory author's interpretive name. The research did not establish that Thucydides presents the Melian episode itself as a doctrine of *kairos*.

The fourth intervention concerns **Dependencia**. Prebisch's 1949 Spanish text, revised in English in 1950, is a strong anchor for centre-periphery structuralism and the proposition that the international division of labour can create persistent asymmetries. It should not be presented as if it already contained the full body of later dependency theory. Cardoso and Faletto, among others, developed accounts in which dependency interacted with internal political coalitions and could coexist with forms of industrial development. Duvall's conceptual review makes the larger point explicit: “dependence” and *dependencia* have been used for materially different arguments. citeturn18search1turn18search8turn17search2turn17search0

Across the product, each historical note should therefore distinguish four statuses:

| Status | Meaning in the editorial layer |
| --- | --- |
| **Historical fact** | A claim directly supported by a primary text, official record, or well-established historical source |
| **Scholarly interpretation** | An attributed or well-supported reading of what the text, practice or episode means |
| **Authored analogy** | The inventory's own comparison between that history and an archetype dimension |
| **Current-policy claim** | A claim about present institutions or political rhetoric; it must never be smuggled in through a historical analogy |

That last distinction is especially important for **Shi (勢)**. Official Chinese discourse still uses 勢 and 大势 in phrases concerning changing conditions, historical trends and judgements about the international environment. That demonstrates contemporary lexical use. It does not establish an unbroken Sunzian doctrine, prove that a current policy is “Shi”, or permit the inventory to infer how any present Chinese actor thinks. citeturn5search11turn2search10turn2search7

## Structured archetype evidence records

**P+ | Kairos**

**Historical analogue and relevance:** *Thucydides, Melian Dialogue, Book V, 84–116, 416 BCE.* The relevant feature is the radical power asymmetry between Athens and Melos and the Athenian insistence that bargaining cannot be separated from relative capability. A scholarly Cambridge translation should replace the unattributed or lightly attributed web translations commonly used online. citeturn2search5 The inventory's additional emphasis on acting when an opening exists is a defensible analogy to power-sensitive strategy, but it is **authored analogy**, not a direct claim that the Melian Dialogue expounds a theory called *kairos*.

**Why the analogy works:** The episode puts material asymmetry at the centre of the negotiations. It therefore provides a memorable hard case for a result whose baseline wording says that power and timing come first. The analogy is strongest for **power under unequal bargaining conditions**. fileciteturn3file0L2-L2

**Strongest objection:** Melos is too extreme to serve as an uncomplicated realist exemplar. The Athenians destroy Melian autonomy after negotiations fail, and the dialogue appears in a wider narrative that shortly moves into the disastrous Sicilian expedition. The Stanford Encyclopedia's treatment of realism explicitly warns against treating the Athenian speakers as a transparent statement of Thucydides' own view; Lebow and Kelly read the episode within a decline from legitimate hegemony toward coercive empire; newer classical scholarship likewise emphasizes the indictment embedded in the dialogue. citeturn4search1turn4search7turn4search8

**Contested terminology:** `Kairos` should be labelled an **authored archetype name**. The present research did not validate a sufficiently strong classical lexicographical source tying *kairos* specifically to the Melian episode. It is therefore unsafe to say that “the Melian Dialogue is an example of the Greek strategic concept of kairos”. The product may use *Kairos* as its own mnemonic for timing, provided that distinction is visible.

**Canonical evidence set:** `THU-2013`, `SEP-REALISM`, `LEBOW-KELLY-2001`, `JAMES-2024`. The first is the primary ancient narrative in scholarly translation. The second contextualizes its use in realist IR. The latter two supply the strongest corrective against reading Athenian claims as Thucydides' uncomplicated endorsement. citeturn2search5turn4search1turn4search7turn4search8

**Facts versus interpretations:** It is historical/textual fact that Thucydides stages negotiations between Athens and Melos during the war and foregrounds the parties' unequal position. It is scholarly interpretation that the dialogue is an indictment of Athenian imperial degeneration. It is authored analogy that this episode exemplifies the inventory's “strategic opening” or timing component. It would be an unsupported interpretation to state simply that Thucydides was a realist who endorsed the Athenian argument. citeturn4search1turn4search7

**Unresolved research gaps:** an authoritative classical-language reference for the semantic range of *kairos* relevant to politics and strategy; verification of whether a leading Thucydides commentary uses *kairos* as an interpretive category for V.84–116; a stable Cambridge Core locator for the Mynott chapter.

**Do not publish without qualification:** “Thucydides teaches that the strong should dominate the weak”; “the Melian Dialogue proves that power always overrides morality”; “Kairos was the Athenian strategy at Melos”; “Melos is a neutral example of realism”; “Thucydides endorsed the Athenian position.”

**Recommended editorial line:** *The Melian Dialogue is an extreme analogue for bargaining under unequal power. It captures the importance of capability better than it captures timing, and Thucydides' wider narrative also supports readings that condemn Athenian overreach.*

**P- | Shi (勢)**

**Historical analogue and relevance:** *Sunzi, The Art of War.* Roger T. Ames's scholarly translation is a substantially better evidentiary anchor than the present eNotes excerpt. Ames translates and discusses *shi* within a larger strategic vocabulary concerning positional advantage and the conditions from which effective action becomes possible. His Routledge Encyclopedia treatment provides a second scholarly anchor. citeturn5search3turn5search7

**Why the analogy works:** The inventory's wording describes power as a position built over time, with an emphasis on establishing favourable conditions before forcing an outcome. That is a reasonable editorial synthesis of one important strand of Sunzi. But Sunzi should not be reduced to “avoid direct confrontation”. Lisa Raphals notes that the text's direct and indirect categories are relational and situational rather than an absolute preference for one over the other. citeturn5search2

**Strongest objection:** `勢` is too semantically rich to equal any single English phrase. Renderings such as “strategic advantage”, “configuration”, “disposition”, “potential” or the effectiveness produced by circumstances capture different aspects. An archetype description that turns *shi* into patience, deception, indirection or accumulated leverage alone would create false precision. Ames and Raphals support a reading centred on strategically configured conditions, but not an invariant rule of indirectness. citeturn5search3turn5search2

**Contested terminology and modern Chinese use:** The lexical forms 勢 and 大势 remain active in contemporary official Chinese political language. Official commentary uses formulations such as judging or grasping the “大势”, and recent official media language also deploys 勢 to describe broad trends and favourable or unfavourable conditions. citeturn5search11turn2search10turn2search7 That evidence establishes **current language use only**. It does not establish conceptual continuity with Sunzi, a unified contemporary doctrine, or an archetype assignment for the PRC.

The safest product note is: *“Shi is a classical strategic term with a wider semantic range than any one English translation. 勢 and 大势 also appear in modern Chinese political language. This result uses the classical strategic analogy and makes no claim about contemporary Chinese policy.”*

**Canonical evidence set:** `AMES-1993`, `AMES-REP-1998`, `RAPHALS-2016`, `CAC-2025`. The first three support classical interpretation. `CAC-2025` has a deliberately narrower review status, `verified-current-language-use-only`. citeturn5search3turn5search7turn5search2turn5search11

**Facts versus interpretations:** It is textual/scholarly fact that *shi* appears as an important term in Sunzi and that scholars dispute how best to render its conceptual range. It is scholarly interpretation that configured advantage is a useful umbrella for that range. It is authored analogy that the inventory maps *shi* onto “build your position before forcing the outcome”. It is a current-language fact that 勢/大势 appears in present official rhetoric. Any claim that modern Chinese foreign policy is “Sunzian Shi” is **withheld**.

**Unresolved research gaps:** a second independently verified critical Sunzi translation for a translation-comparison note; a critical textual-history source for the dating and composition of the received *Sunzi*; the stable official URL for the selected 2025 Chinese-language example. The present app's “5th C. BC” analogue date should not be treated as settled until the textual-dating issue is independently sourced. fileciteturn3file0L2-L2

**Do not publish without qualification:** “Shi means indirect power”; “Sunzi says never confront an adversary directly”; “Shi means patience”; “modern Chinese strategy is based on Shi”; “大势 is the modern political meaning of Sunzi's 勢”; “the Sunzi was definitively written in the fifth century BCE.”

**Recommended editorial line:** *Shi (勢) points to the advantage created by position, configuration and changing conditions. The term has several classical meanings and remains live in modern Chinese vocabulary; the archetype does not treat contemporary usage as evidence of a Sunzian state doctrine.*

**R+ | Grotian**

**Historical analogue and relevance:** Hugo Grotius, *De Jure Belli ac Pacis*, first published in 1625. Grotius argues that war and relations among political communities remain subject to law and obligation. His argument draws on natural law as well as rules arising through the practice or consent of peoples. His work therefore makes sense as an analogue for a result that believes international rules can have real normative force. citeturn7search3turn7search11turn7search12

**Why the analogy works:** The strongest link is the proposition that politics between states is not a law-free domain. Grotius also discusses limits on permissible reasons for war and obligations of moderation. He does not reduce international conduct to prudence or superior force. citeturn7search7turn7search8turn7search10

**Strongest objection:** The current inventory gloss is too enforcement-forward. At baseline it says that rules have force “only when they are backed by credible enforcement”. fileciteturn3file0L2-L2 That proposition should not be attributed to Grotius. His legal theory does not make the validity of natural law or the law of nations depend solely on institutional enforcement. Hedley Bull's classic account of Grotius instead emphasizes international society, universal law and a historically important form of solidarism, while also noting the very limited international institutional machinery available to Grotius. citeturn6search3

**Contested terminology:** `Grotian` has at least two relevant layers. First is **Grotius's own corpus**. Second is a retrospective IR tradition. Martin Wight's three traditions, commonly rendered Hobbesian/realist, Grotian/rationalist and Kantian/revolutionist, place “Grotian” in a rationalist middle tradition concerned with international society. citeturn6search12 Bull's use overlaps but is not identical to either Grotius's own thought or every later English School formulation. Calling an enforcement-maximizing pole “Grotian” therefore creates a real semantic collision.

The frozen name can survive, but the disclosure should say: *“The label points to Grotius's insistence that law applies between political communities. English School scholars later used ‘Grotian’ more broadly for a tradition of international society. The archetype's enforcement dimension is an authored extension, not a summary of Grotius's legal theory.”*

**Canonical evidence set:** `GROTIUS-2005`, `BULL-1992`, `KEENE-2020`. citeturn7search3turn6search3turn6search12

**Facts versus interpretations:** It is textual fact that Grotius gives natural law and the law of nations roles in regulating war. It is intellectual-history fact that later IR thinkers constructed a “Grotian” tradition around ideas of international society. Exactly where Grotius belongs on solidarist/pluralist or enforcement dimensions is scholarly interpretation. Mapping him onto the inventory's R+ enforcement pole is **authored analogy**.

**Unresolved research gaps:** whether the public-facing R+ gloss can be rewritten without affecting frozen instrument semantics; a direct primary passage appropriate for a short source drawer on enforcement and punishment, rather than law generally; whether the product wants its terminology note to distinguish Wight, Bull and Grotius individually or just warn of the collision.

**Do not publish without qualification:** “Grotius believed law matters only when it is enforced”; “Grotian means enforcement-backed international order”; “Grotius designed the modern rules-based international order”; “the English School's Grotian tradition is the same thing as this R+ pole.”

**Recommended editorial line:** *Grotius is relevant because he argued that law and obligation continue to apply across political borders and during war. Later scholars made “Grotian” a wider tradition of international society. The inventory's emphasis on enforcement is a modern analytical extension.*

**R- | Concert**

**Historical analogue and relevance:** The 1815 Vienna settlement and the subsequent Concert of Europe. The official French text of the Congress's Final Act survives in a digitized 1815 edition. It codified a major territorial and political settlement after the Napoleonic Wars. citeturn19search3 The subsequent Concert involved practices of great-power consultation and management rather than a single permanent organisation. Elrod's classic study identifies consultation and restraint as central features; Rendall likewise emphasizes self-restraint alongside balance-of-power incentives. citeturn19search6turn19search0

**Why the analogy works:** The inventory's R- wording says durable cooperation rests on powerful actors accepting rules and restraint. That is a strong fit with the Concert as an example of major powers trying to manage rivalry through consultation, common expectations and restraint without a universal enforcement authority. fileciteturn3file0L2-L2 Raymond's analysis usefully describes the early system as a practice of “great power management”, including the development of procedural rules around consultation. citeturn19search15

**Strongest objection:** The Concert was not egalitarian multilateralism. Its central political privilege belonged to great powers. Smaller states and populations subject to imperial rule did not receive equal influence. Nor should the nineteenth century be presented as a seamless “long peace”. Anderson documents the analytical distortions produced when the period is viewed only through the absence of some types of great-power war while overlooking other European wars, repression and imperial violence. citeturn19search12

**Contested terminology:** `Congress of Vienna`, `Congress System`, and `Concert of Europe` overlap but are not synonyms. Vienna was a settlement conference and body of agreements. The post-1815 congresses and later Concert were evolving practices. Scholars disagree on periodisation, on how institutionalized the Concert really was, and on how much stability resulted from concert procedures rather than material balance, common regime interests or post-Napoleonic exhaustion. Elrod and Rendall represent different but compatible emphases within that debate. citeturn19search6turn19search0

**Canonical evidence set:** `VIENNA-1815`, `ELROD-1976`, `RENDALL-2006`, `RAYMOND-2019`, `ANDERSON-2007`. citeturn19search3turn19search6turn19search0turn19search15turn19search12

**Facts versus interpretations:** It is historical fact that the great powers negotiated the Vienna settlement and subsequently used conferences and consultation in managing European affairs. It is scholarly interpretation that this practice materially increased restraint or caused the relative stability of the system. It is authored analogy that this historical arrangement represents the inventory's R- preference for consent and restraint among powerful actors. Calling it a successful model for contemporary global governance would be a separate **current-policy argument**, not historical fact.

**Unresolved research gaps:** exact periodisation to use in the source drawer; whether to label the analogue “Vienna settlement and Concert practice” instead of only “Congress of Vienna”; a primary treaty/conference source for the later consultation mechanism in addition to the Vienna Final Act.

**Do not publish without qualification:** “The Congress of Vienna created the Concert of Europe”; “the Concert kept Europe at peace for a century”; “all European states participated on equal terms”; “the Concert was an early United Nations”; “the Concert shows that great-power consensus is sufficient for legitimate order.”

**Recommended editorial line:** *The Concert illustrates order through consultation and restraint among major powers. Its stability came with hierarchy and exclusion, and historians disagree over how much the Concert itself explains nineteenth-century peace.*

**M+ | Satyagraha**

**Historical analogue and relevance:** The 1930 Salt Satyagraha. Archival material collected by the Gandhi Heritage Portal dates Gandhi's pre-march correspondence to early March 1930 and the march from 12 March; an official Indian heritage project records the culminating breach of the salt law on 6 April. citeturn12search0turn12search1 The episode joined organized non-cooperation, deliberate lawbreaking, symbolism and mass political communication around a tangible colonial monopoly.

**Why the analogy works:** The inventory's M+ result concerns the political effects of beliefs, legitimacy and public meaning. The Salt Satyagraha provides a historically concrete example of a political campaign turning an ordinary rule and commodity into a wider argument about authority. The later IR literature on norm emergence provides one conceptual lens for understanding challenges to accepted standards, while Acharya's localization framework warns that new norms are reconstructed through local actors rather than simply diffused intact. citeturn13search1turn13search0

**Strongest objection:** The analogy can become too clean in two ways. First, Finnemore and Sikkink's norm life-cycle and Acharya's localization theory are later scholarly frameworks. Gandhi did not conduct the Salt March according to an IR “norm cascade” model. Second, satyagraha was a political method embedded in a much larger anti-colonial struggle. Reducing it to “changing the narrative” risks understating organization, sacrifice, repression, material grievances and bargaining.

**Contested terminology and biography:** `Satyagraha` should not be flattened into a generic synonym for peaceful protest. Scholarship on Gandhi's intellectual development shows that the relationship among satyagraha, *ahimsa*, political technique and moral philosophy changed over time. citeturn14search0 The archetype must also distinguish method from biography. Gandhi's writings and politics on caste and race have been subjects of continuing critique and reassessment, including scholarship that reads those issues directly against the subsequent iconic status of satyagraha. citeturn14search4 The named analogue therefore cannot serve as an endorsement of Gandhi as a person.

**Canonical evidence set:** `GANDHI-SALT`, `HAZAMA-2022`, `FINN-SIKK-1998`, `ACHARYA-2004`, `MADDIPATI-2024`. citeturn12search0turn14search0turn13search1turn13search0turn14search4

**Facts versus interpretations:** It is historical fact that the Salt Satyagraha deliberately challenged British salt law in 1930. It is intellectual-history fact that *satyagraha* developed as a distinctive term and practice over Gandhi's career. It is scholarly interpretation that the campaign's political effects can be understood through legitimacy or norm contestation. Mapping the Salt March onto the inventory's belief-and-narrative dimension is **authored analogy**. Claims that narrative change alone produced Indian independence are **withheld**.

**Unresolved research gaps:** selection of a single primary Gandhi text that defines *satyagraha* in his own words and can be cited with stable volume/page metadata; whether the evidence drawer should separately include Ambedkar's 1936 critique on caste; a specialist source on the immediate political consequences of the Salt campaign that avoids retrospective hagiography.

**Do not publish without qualification:** “Satyagraha means non-violent protest”; “the Salt March succeeded by changing the narrative”; “Gandhi proved that moral persuasion defeats material power”; “this archetype endorses Gandhi”; “Gandhi's politics were uniformly egalitarian”; “Finnemore and Sikkink explain what Gandhi was consciously doing.”

**Recommended editorial line:** *The Salt Satyagraha is an analogue for contesting the legitimacy of a rule in public. Later norm theory helps interpret that mechanism, but it is not the campaign's own theory, and the archetype refers to a method rather than an endorsement of Gandhi's biography.*

**M- | Musyawarah**

**Historical analogue and relevance:** Indonesian and Malay political vocabulary around `musyawarah` and `mufakat`, compared with ASEAN's practice of consultation and consensus. Kawamura's study of Indonesia describes *musyawarah-mufakat* as a consensus-oriented decision rule with continuing political use and identifies a trade-off between inclusion and decision efficiency. citeturn10search15

**Why the analogy works:** ASEAN's Charter provides a strong institutional analogue. Article 20 states that ASEAN decision-making is, as a basic principle, based on consultation and consensus. Article 2 separately includes non-interference in members' internal affairs and enhanced consultations on matters affecting the common interest. citeturn24view0turn24view1 This supports an archetype centred on legitimacy achieved through patient consultation rather than immediate imposition.

**Strongest objection:** ASEAN consultation is not identical to *musyawarah-mufakat*. Nor is consensus absolute. Article 20 itself says that when consensus cannot be achieved, the ASEAN Summit may decide how a specific decision is to be made. citeturn24view1 Scholarship also finds that ASEAN's consensus procedures can yield diluted or non-binding outcomes and that the content of “non-interference” varies with political circumstances. citeturn10search13turn10search18

**Contested terminology and origins:** A purely “indigenous Malay/Indonesian” origin claim is too simple. Indonesian scholarship on *syura* and *musyawarah* places the term in an Arabic and Islamic lexical-conceptual lineage, while the *musyawarah-mufakat* decision practice became locally embedded in Indonesian political and social institutions. citeturn20search2turn10search15 The safest description is therefore **localized Malay/Indonesian practice drawing on a wider Islamic lexical tradition**, not an untouched ancient custom uniquely owned by one modern state.

The current analogue year `1967` also needs qualification. That is the founding year of ASEAN, not evidence that a fully articulated doctrine called “the ASEAN Way” or an ASEAN-wide institution called *musyawarah* was codified in 1967. The authoritative Charter formulation cited here dates from the Charter first published in 2007, in the 2015 ASEAN Secretariat reprint reviewed for this report. citeturn24view0turn24view1

**Canonical evidence set:** `ASEAN-CHARTER-2015`, `KAWAMURA-2011`, `SOHRAH-2015`, `CHIOU-2010`, `JONES-2010`. citeturn24view0turn24view1turn10search15turn20search2turn10search13turn10search18

**Facts versus interpretations:** It is institutional fact that ASEAN's Charter gives consultation and consensus a privileged role and includes non-interference among ASEAN principles. It is historical/intellectual fact that *musyawarah-mufakat* has an Indonesian political history and that the vocabulary has Islamic/Arabic roots. It is scholarly interpretation that these practices form a coherent “ASEAN Way”, and scholarship contests how consistently those norms actually govern behaviour. Mapping them to M- is **authored analogy**.

**Unresolved research gaps:** a specialist Malay-language historical source tracing use of *musyawarah* and *mufakat* before modern Indonesian state institutions; a preferred Indonesian lexicographic source; a rigorous genealogy of when “ASEAN Way” became common as a self-description versus scholarly label; whether the application wants a contemporary Myanmar/Five-Point Consensus example of consensus paralysis. That last item would be a current-policy illustration and should remain outside the archetype definition.

**Do not publish without qualification:** “Musyawarah is an ancient indigenous ASEAN tradition”; “ASEAN decides everything unanimously”; “the ASEAN Way began in 1967”; “musyawarah and the ASEAN Way are the same thing”; “non-interference prevents ASEAN from acting”; “all ASEAN states share the same consensus culture.”

**Recommended editorial line:** *Musyawarah is a localized tradition of deliberation oriented toward agreement. ASEAN's consultation-and-consensus practice is a useful regional analogue, but it is neither identical to musyawarah-mufakat nor an absolute unanimity rule.*

**S+ | Dirigisme**

**Historical analogue and relevance:** France's first Monnet Plan. The French government created the Commissariat général du Plan in January 1946 and appointed Jean Monnet as its first commissioner. citeturn15search7turn15search3 The Jean Monnet Foundation's archival holdings preserve the first commissioner's 16 March 1946 report and draft planning documents from April 1946. citeturn15search0turn15search5

**Why the analogy works:** The early plan concentrated modernization and investment attention on a limited set of bottleneck sectors. A contemporary November 1946 account lists coal, electricity, steel, cement, agricultural machinery and internal transport among the basic branches receiving production objectives. The same account explicitly distinguished the French plan from Soviet-style comprehensive command planning and stated that private enterprise remained part of the system. citeturn15search2 This is a strong analogue for **selective state direction of investment and productive capacity**.

**Strongest objection:** The Monnet Plan arose under exceptional conditions. France faced reconstruction, physical scarcity, major institutional change and external financing needs after the Second World War. The archival record itself contains files on US assistance and Washington loan negotiations, while Brunet's archival reassessment shows how deeply the Plan's creation was bound to the specific domestic political and bureaucratic context of 1945–46. citeturn15search1turn16search0 It therefore cannot demonstrate what planning will accomplish in a normal peacetime economy with different political coalitions, capital markets or fiscal constraints.

**Markets and ownership:** `Dirigisme` should not be defined as state ownership. Indicative planning can direct credit, coordinate investment, prioritize sectors and set targets while substantial market exchange and private enterprise continue. Conversely, postwar France also contained important nationalized sectors. The historical economy was mixed. The analogue is about **direction and coordination**, not one ownership form. citeturn15search2turn16search0

**Contested terminology:** The baseline evidence currently says “The Monnet Plan is dirigisme in its own language”. fileciteturn2file0L2-L2 That sentence should be withdrawn. The primary institutional language verified here is the language of the *Plan*, *modernisation* and the *Commissariat général du Plan*. The research did not verify a first-plan primary document in which Monnet's institution self-identifies its programme as `dirigisme`. `Dirigisme` is safer as the inventory's later analytical label.

**External aid:** US assistance and external borrowing were part of the planning context, and the Monnet archive explicitly contains material concerning American aid and loan negotiations. citeturn15search1turn15search5 This report does **not** supply a percentage of Monnet Plan investment financed through Marshall aid or other US funds because that exact quantitative claim was not independently reconciled across authoritative sources. It remains `research-required`.

**Canonical evidence set:** `LEGIFRANCE-1946`, `MONNET-AMF-1946`, `LEMONDE-1946`, `BRUNET-2018`, `HCSP-2026`. citeturn15search7turn15search0turn15search2turn16search0turn15search3

**Facts versus interpretations:** It is historical fact that France established a planning commissariat and adopted selective production and modernization objectives. It is historical fact that the arrangement did not abolish private enterprise. It is scholarly interpretation how much its effectiveness depended on institutional autonomy, political bargains, nationalization or external capital. Calling the package `Dirigisme` and mapping it onto S+ are **authored analytical choices**.

**Unresolved research gaps:** exact US/Marshall-aid financing share; sector-by-sector ownership and credit-control data suitable for a concise source drawer; whether first-plan production objectives were formally mandatory, negotiated, indicative or differently binding across sectors; a primary usage history of the term *dirigisme* itself.

**Do not publish without qualification:** “The Monnet Plan nationalized the French economy”; “private firms were ordered to meet government production quotas across the economy”; “dirigisme means state ownership”; “the Monnet Plan proved that state planning outperforms markets”; “the Monnet Plan called itself dirigisme”; “Marshall aid financed X per cent of the Plan” until the quantitative record is reconciled.

**Recommended editorial line:** *The Monnet Plan is an analogue for selective state direction of investment and productive capacity. It coordinated a mixed economy under exceptional reconstruction conditions; it was neither laissez-faire nor comprehensive command planning.*

**S- | Dependencia**

**Historical analogue and relevance:** Raúl Prebisch's 1949 Spanish report, *El desarrollo económico de la América Latina y algunos de sus principales problemas*, and its revised 1950 English edition. CEPAL's archival record dates the Spanish document to 14 May 1949 under UN symbol `E/CN.12/89`; the English revision appeared in 1950 as `E/CN.12/89/Rev.1`. citeturn18search1turn18search8 The text became a foundational statement of the centre-periphery approach associated with Latin American structuralism. CEPAL's own retrospective account emphasizes centre-periphery relations, the problems associated with dependence on primary exports, and an active development role for the state. citeturn18search0

**Why the analogy works:** The strongest fit is structural. Prebisch asks how a country's position in an international economic structure shapes development options and the distribution of gains. That is closely aligned with an S- result concerned with dependencies, external vulnerability and constrained room for action. citeturn18search1turn18search0

**Strongest objection:** `Prebisch = dependencia` is historically too compressed. Prebisch's structuralism predates and differs from several later dependency schools. Cardoso and Faletto, for example, examine how external constraints interact with domestic political and social structures and explicitly allow for historically varying forms of development under dependency. citeturn17search2turn17search5 Duvall's conceptual survey further shows that the dependency literature contains different concepts, causal mechanisms and levels of analysis. citeturn17search0

**Policy implications:** Prebisch's centre-periphery analysis helped justify industrialization, structural transformation and active public policy. It did not amount to a universal prescription for autarky or withdrawal from international trade. The product's existing gloss, “reducing external dependence is central to resilience”, is therefore best understood as an **authored extension from structural vulnerability**, not a complete summary of Prebisch's policy programme. fileciteturn3file0L2-L2 citeturn18search0

**Contested terminology:** `Dependencia` is a broad intellectual label, not a single theorem. Prebischian/ECLAC structuralism, Cardoso and Faletto's historical sociology, and other dependency approaches should not be collapsed into a single claim about trade or external exposure. Duvall's title and argument are especially useful for the source drawer because they make conceptual imprecision itself part of the history of the literature. citeturn17search0

The baseline year `1950` is defensible for the English version, but the original Spanish document is dated 1949. The source layer should show both rather than implying the idea began only in 1950. citeturn18search1turn18search8

**Canonical evidence set:** `PREBISCH-1949`, `PREBISCH-1950`, `CARDOSO-FALETTO-1979`, `DUVALL-1978`. citeturn18search1turn18search8turn17search2turn17search0

**Facts versus interpretations:** It is historical/textual fact that Prebisch developed a centre-periphery analysis and argued for structural change in Latin American development. It is intellectual-history fact that later dependency scholarship diversified beyond Prebisch. It is scholarly interpretation how direct the genealogy from structuralism to particular dependency schools should be drawn. Making “resilience through reduced dependence” the defining S- orientation is **authored analogy**.

**Unresolved research gaps:** a Portuguese-language canonical dependency source for the Brazilian branch of the literature; a direct comparison of Prebisch, André Gunder Frank and Cardoso/Faletto suitable for a three-sentence source drawer; whether “centre-periphery” or “dependency” should appear first in the public explanatory note.

**Do not publish without qualification:** “Prebisch founded dependency theory”; “dependencia says poor countries should leave world markets”; “dependency theory prescribes autarky”; “centre-periphery theory predicts permanent underdevelopment”; “all dependencia scholars agreed on the same mechanism”; “reducing trade dependence is Prebisch's central prescription.”

**Recommended editorial line:** *Prebisch's centre-periphery analysis is the historical anchor for this archetype. Later dependencia theories developed different mechanisms and political arguments, so the name signals a family resemblance rather than one unified doctrine.*
